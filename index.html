A routine examination, request or loss of control can carry a different meaning for someone with a history of trauma.

Look beyond the presenting task

What else might be going on?

A young person may be in hospital for a physical health reason while also coping with distress that changes how they communicate, tolerate care or respond to people around them.

The behaviour you see may not be about the task you are there to complete.
Anxious or distressed feelingsFear, uncertainty, loss of control or overwhelm.
Self-injurious behaviourCurrent or recent self-harm may sit alongside the medical presentation.
Mental ill healthDistress or illness may be masked, overlooked or difficult to express.
Substance useIntoxication, withdrawal or related distress can affect presentation and risk.
Trauma and shamePast experiences can shape how a young person reads our words, tone and actions.
Learning, communication or sensory needsUnderstanding and expression may require more time, adaptation and checking.
Acute mental-health guidance emphasises that physical and mental-health needs can occur together and that distress, self-harm, substance misuse and learning needs may be relevant in acute hospital care.

Reflection for medical staff

A brief interaction can have a longer impact

Your contact may be brief.
Its impact may not be.

A task-focused interaction can end for the medic while the young person is still processing what happened — and ward colleagues remain alongside them.

Before you leave, ask yourself:
  • What state was the young person in when I arrived?
  • What state might they be in when I leave?
  • What might colleagues need to manage afterwards?

Reflective practice

How might a young person view a medic?

Possible perception

A young person may experience medics as powerful or controlling because they are making decisions about treatment, including diet and food intake.

Why this matters

Changes to diet can feel highly anxiety-provoking. A brief or dismissive interaction may leave them feeling unheard, powerless, out of control and less involved in care.

Possible impact

They may try to regain control through further eating disorder behaviours, or be less likely to follow a plan that feels imposed on them.

Link to theory: this connects to the attitude and behaviour cycle. Staff attitudes and behaviours can influence the young person’s attitude and response, escalating or de-escalating the situation.

Necessary decisions still need compassionate communication

Give voice, even when the outcome cannot change

How we communicate a necessary treatment decision can be just as important as the decision itself. We may not always be able to give the young person the outcome they want, but we can still give them a voice, listen to their concerns, explain decisions and offer meaningful choices wherever possible.
25
Self-harm

Looking beyond the behaviour

“Are they just doing it for attention?”

Self-harm should not be dismissed as simply attention-seeking. Even when someone is trying to communicate distress or seek attention, consider how difficult things must feel for this to seem like the only way they can show that they need help.

They may not yet have the words, confidence, relationships or alternative coping strategies to ask for help in another way.

Why might someone self-harm?

  • Cope with overwhelming emotional distress
  • Regulate or release intense emotions
  • Express feelings that are difficult to put into words
  • Create a sense of control
  • Cope with numbness or disconnection
  • Punish themselves because of guilt, shame or low self-worth
  • Manage traumatic experiences or painful memories
  • Communicate distress or a need for support
  • Cope with interpersonal difficulties, rejection or conflict
  • Temporarily distract from emotional pain
Self-harm does not necessarily mean that someone is trying to end their life, although suicidal thoughts and risk should always be taken seriously.
26
Accessing healthcare after self-harm

Attitudes can be communicated through behaviour

Someone requiring treatment after self-harm may already feel ashamed, frightened, vulnerable or worthless.

Now imagine encountering a professional whose words, tone or body language communicate: “Why should I help you? You did this to yourself.”

Even when those words are never spoken, attitudes can still be communicated through our behaviour.
